# Turning an input into an infographic

The user rarely says "build me a decision tree". They hand over a post, a link or a
sentence and expect you to work out what the picture should be. The move is always the
same: find the one argument worth drawing, then pick the shape that carries it.

## The question that decides the template

**Does the image repeat the claim, or does it show the mechanism?**

Repeating the claim is a quote card. Showing the mechanism is a cheat sheet, a decision
tree or a stack. Both are legitimate and they trade off against each other:

- The **claim** version stops the scroll and sends people to the caption. It teaches
  nothing on its own, so it lives or dies on the hook.
- The **mechanism** version stands alone, gets saved and reshared, and travels past the
  original audience. It also gives the answer away, which weakens the reason to click
  through to a video or a link.

Build one of each. Both go on the canvas, every time, even when one looks obviously right
to you: the user knows their audience and their goal for this particular post, and seeing
the two side by side takes them a second where imagining the other one takes a paragraph
from you and still leaves them guessing.

Say the tradeoff out loud for both, then name your pick. A set where only your favourite
gets an argument is a rigged vote.

## By input type

### LinkedIn or X copy

Everything you need is in the post. The **hook** is the quote card. The **body**, if it
carries a numbered list, a stack of tools or a set of rules, is the cheat sheet. If the
body is an if-this-then-that rule, it is a decision tree.

Lift the hook nearly verbatim for a quote card. It was already written to stop a scroll,
and rewriting it usually weakens it. Trim it to three short paragraphs.

### A YouTube link

Fetch the transcript before designing. A visual built from the title is a guess, and the
argument that makes a good infographic is almost never in the title.

Look for the diagram the creator drew in words. Videos that explain a system usually
contain a spoken walkthrough of its layers or steps: that is the infographic. Also look for
the correction, the "most people do X, and that is why it does not work" beat, because a
mechanism drawing lands hardest when it is answering a mistake.

Numbers in a transcript are real and quotable. Numbers you infer are not.

### A Reddit post or thread

Reddit rewards the mechanism over the claim, and it punishes anything that reads as an ad.
Prefer the cheat sheet or the decision tree, keep the branding to the quiet domain line,
and make sure the image is genuinely useful without the caption.

### A raw brief or one-line idea

Ask what the reader should be able to do differently after seeing it. If the answer is
"agree with me", it is a quote card. If the answer is "apply this to their own situation",
it is a decision tree. If the answer is "know what the pieces are", it is a cheat sheet or
a stack.

If the brief is too thin to answer that, say so and ask one question rather than inventing
substance.

## Writing copy for an artboard

Post copy and artboard copy are not the same thing. Artboard copy has to survive being read
at thumbnail size and out of context.

- **Shorter than you think.** A cheat sheet body line is one sentence, around twelve words.
  A quote card paragraph is eight to twelve.
- **One idea per block.** If a block needs a comma-spliced second thought, split it or cut
  it.
- **Concrete nouns.** "Monday numbers" beats "reporting workflows". "The same question,
  again" beats "repetitive inbound queries".
- **Sentence case, no em dashes,** and no hype vocabulary: transform, revolutionize,
  game-changing, unlock, leverage, seamless, robust, supercharge.
- **Bracket every number you cannot source.** `[0h]` is honest. A made-up percentage is not,
  and an image outlives the caption that would have hedged it.

## After it is built

Hand over the canvas link with a line on each option and its tradeoff, then export the PNG
the user picks. Name the placeholders explicitly. If the copy makes a claim that was true
when the source was recorded, say that it is worth re-checking before posting: a stat that
was accurate in a video from three months ago may not be accurate today.
